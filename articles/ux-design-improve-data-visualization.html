How UX Design Can Improve Your Data Visualization.

Data visualization simplifies information by building structures and patterns that are easier for human brains to understand. Gestalt principles, hierarchy, and accessibility remove complexity and guide users toward meaningful interpretation.

N
Nicolle Rodrigues UX Designer & Design Engineer

Data visualization simplifies information by building structures and patterns that are easier for human brains to understand. UX principles around Gestalt, hierarchy, and accessibility remove complexity and guide users toward meaningful interpretation.

Taking complex data and breaking it down into something that's easier to understand should be one of your top priorities as a designer. The right structure helps people scan, scan again, and act. The wrong one leaves them guessing.

Understand your data

Taking complex data and breaking it down into something that is easier to understand should be one of your top priorities. Collecting the right information is the foundation of visual representation.

Start by checking your data sources and get familiar with the content. The more you understand the data, the easier it will be to recreate it visually, and the more likely your visualization is to tell a story your audience can actually use.

Learn Gestalt principles

Gestalt is German for “unified whole.” The first principles were articulated around 1920 by German psychologists Max Wertheimer, Kurt Koffka, and Wolfgang Kohler. Their goal was to understand how humans process and select meaningful information given all of the overwhelming stimuli that surround us.

According to Kelley Gordon, Digital Design Lead at Nielsen Norman Group, “Gestalt Principles explain how humans simplify and organize complex images that consist of many elements by subconsciously arranging the parts into an organized system that creates a whole, rather than interpreting them as a series of disparate elements.”

Use appropriate visualizations

Charts are the most popular way to represent data and an easy way to show time, measurement, and comparison. Bar charts and line graphs can tell a story quickly.

Other visuals such as bubble charts may overcomplicate your content, making it harder to understand. Pick the visualization that fits the question your audience is actually asking, not the one that looks most impressive on a slide.

Ensure useful scale and proportion

Effective data visualization should give users quick and accurate information at a glance. Complex datasets should emphasize accuracy, and it's important to make sure your charts aren't visually busy so the message doesn't get lost.

Pie charts are best when each section is equally important. Donut charts are great to highlight portions of data while giving an additional opportunity for storytelling at the center. Use the wrong proportion for either and you can mislead a reader without realizing it.

Consider accessibility

Accessibility of visuals to those with disabilities is commonly overlooked, but there's a lot you can do to make sure your data is accessible to everyone. Avoid generalities and be descriptive in both titles and labels on your graphs. Use high quality images that won't get pixelated, and pay attention to color choices.

WCAG 2.0 level AA requires a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text. Converting your graphics to grayscale can help you identify how much contrast your color selection actually has.

Designing data people can use

When focusing on representing data, UX design is a powerful tool to make your visualization stand out and effectively communicate complex information to your audience. Data visualization expert Edward R. Tufte highlights the functionality of UX combined with data when he states: “The essential test of design is how well it assists the understanding of the content, not how stylish it is.”

Good data design quietly disappears into clarity. Bad data design draws attention to itself. Aim for the first.
